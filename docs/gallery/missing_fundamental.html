    The Missing Fundamental

    ← Back to Gallery

    Topic: Pitch perception, harmonic structure, and the cepstrum — why a tone keeps its pitch even when its fundamental frequency is deleted. Chapter: 2 — Frequency Domain

    Background

    Play a middle C (C4) on a piano and you hear a pitch at its fundamental, f_1 \approx 261.6 Hz, coloured by a comb of harmonics 2f_1, 3f_1, 4f_1, \dots. Now delete that fundamental entirely — remove all energy at f_1 and keep only the harmonics — and something surprising happens: the pitch does not change. You still hear the same C4. This is the missing fundamental (also called residue pitch or virtual pitch).

    It is not a laboratory curiosity — you hear it constantly:

    • A telephone passes only \sim\!300–3400 Hz, cutting the \sim\!100–150 Hz fundamental of a male voice, yet the voice keeps its pitch.
    • Laptop and phone speakers cannot physically move enough air to reproduce bass notes, but music still sounds like it has a bass line.
    • Pipe-organ builders exploit it deliberately (the resultant bass): two higher pipes create the sensation of a much lower note that no pipe actually sounds.

    Key idea

    The pitch we perceive tracks the periodicity of the waveform, not the presence of energy at f_1. A harmonic complex built from partials at 2f_1, 3f_1, 4f_1,\dots is still periodic with period T = 1/f_1 — the greatest common divisor of the partial frequencies is f_1, so the waveform repeats f_1 times a second whether or not the f_1 line is present. The first figure sets up three views of a real C4 piano recording: the waveform repeating on its period T=1/f_1 \approx 3.8 ms, the FFT magnitude as a comb of harmonics, and the cepstrum.

    The frequency domain says the same thing in a dual way. The harmonics are equally spaced by f_1, and that spacing is what encodes the pitch. The cepstrum — the spectrum of the log-spectrum (see Chapter 2) — converts a periodic comb of harmonics into a single peak at quefrency 1/f_1. Because deleting a spectral line barely changes the spacing of the rest, this cepstral peak (and the perceived pitch) is robust to removing the fundamental — the reason cepstral pitch estimation works even on telephone speech.

    The second figure settles the natural objection with an ideal sawtooth at C5 (f_1 \approx 523 Hz) — synthetic rather than recorded, so the partials sit at exact integer multiples of f_1 with clean 1/n amplitudes and “delete the fundamental” and “shift up an octave” are surgically precise. When we delete f_1, the lowest surviving partial sits at 2f_1 \approx 1046 Hz — exactly the fundamental of C6, one octave up. So why isn’t the fundamental-removed tone simply heard as C6? Because pitch follows the spacing of the harmonics, not the lowest frequency present: the f_1-removed sawtooth still has partials every 523 Hz (1046, 1570, 2093,\dots), whereas a genuine C6 has partials every 1046 Hz (1046, 2093, 3139,\dots). Same lowest frequency, different comb — and the ear tracks the comb. (The tell-tale is the partial at 1570 Hz — the odd multiple 3f_1 — present when f_1 is merely removed but absent one octave up.)

    Code

    import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.io.wavfile import read, write as wavwrite

BLUE, RED, GREEN, INK, GREY = '#0d49fb', '#e6091c', '#0a9a4a', '#222222', '#888888'

# ── real C4 recording (figure 1) ────────────────────────────────────────────
def load(name):
    fs, w = read(name)
    x = w[:, 0].astype(float) if w.ndim > 1 else w.astype(float)
    return fs, x / np.max(np.abs(x))
fs, c4 = load('Data/piano_middle_c.wav')
f1 = 261.63                                   # C4 (middle C) fundamental — real recording
f2 = 523.25                                   # C5 — synthetic sawtooth demo (figure 2 + audio)

# ── ideal (band-limited) sawtooth at C5 pitch (figure 2) ────────────────────
dur, N = 2.0, 40
ts = np.arange(int(fs * dur)) / fs
def saw(f0, nmin=1):
    y = np.zeros_like(ts)
    for n in range(nmin, N + 1):
        if n * f0 < 0.45 * fs:
            y += np.sin(2 * np.pi * n * f0 * ts) / n
    return y
saw_full = saw(f2, 1)          # C5: f1, 2f1, 3f1, ...
saw_nof1 = saw(f2, 2)          # fundamental removed: 2f1, 3f1, ...
saw_oct  = saw(2 * f2, 1)      # one octave up (C6): 2f1, 4f1, ...

# ── audio: sawtooth trio (mono int16, normalised, 10 ms fades) ──────────────
def save_wav(name, y):
    y = y / np.max(np.abs(y)); k = int(0.01*fs); r = np.ones_like(y)
    r[:k] = np.linspace(0,1,k); r[-k:] = np.linspace(1,0,k)
    wavwrite(name, fs, (y*r*0.9*32767).astype(np.int16))
save_wav('missing_fundamental_full.wav',   saw_full)
save_wav('missing_fundamental_no_f1.wav',  saw_nof1)
save_wav('missing_fundamental_octave.wav', saw_oct)

# ── analysis helpers (steady segment) ───────────────────────────────────────
i0, L = int(0.3*fs), 16384; win = np.hanning(L)
def spectrum(y):  s = y[i0:i0+L]*win; return np.fft.rfftfreq(L,1/fs), np.abs(np.fft.rfft(s))**2
def magnitude(y): s = y[i0:i0+L]*win; return np.fft.rfftfreq(L,1/fs), np.abs(np.fft.rfft(s))
def cepstrum(y):
    mag = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(y[i0:i0+L]*win)); logX = np.log(mag + 0.01*mag.max())
    c = np.fft.irfft(logX); return np.arange(c.size)/fs, c

# ── FIGURE 1 — real C4 waveform | FFT magnitude | cepstrum ──────────────────
T = 1/f1; t = np.arange(c4.size)/fs
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(11.2, 3.3), layout='constrained')
tw0 = 0.50; twin = (t >= tw0) & (t < tw0 + 0.02)
ax[0].plot((t[twin]-tw0)*1e3, c4[twin], color=INK, lw=1.0)
ax[0].set_title('waveform', fontstyle='italic')
ax[0].set_xlabel('time [ms]'); ax[0].set_ylabel('intensity [arb.]'); ax[0].set_xlim(0, 20)
f, Mg = magnitude(c4); ax[1].plot(f, Mg/Mg.max(), color=INK, lw=0.9)
ax[1].axvline(f1, color=BLUE, ls='--', lw=1.0); ax[1].set_xlim(0,1600); ax[1].set_ylim(0,1.1)
ax[1].set_title('FFT magnitude', fontstyle='italic'); ax[1].set_xlabel('frequency [Hz]')
ax[1].set_ylabel('magnitude [arb.]')
ax[1].text(0.62,0.6,'fundamental\n+ harmonics', transform=ax[1].transAxes, ha='center', fontsize=9)
q, c = cepstrum(c4); m = (q > 1.5e-3) & (q < 0.011)
ax[2].plot(q[m]*1e3, c[m], color=INK, lw=0.9)
ax[2].axvline(T*1e3, color=BLUE, ls='--', lw=1.0); ax[2].set_xlim(1.5,11)
ax[2].set_title('cepstrum', fontstyle='italic'); ax[2].set_xlabel('quefrency [ms]')
ax[2].set_ylabel('amplitude [arb.]'); ax[2].text(T*1e3+0.3, ax[2].get_ylim()[1]*0.8, '$1/f_1$', color=BLUE)
fig.suptitle('Middle C (C4) — waveform, spectrum, cepstrum',
             fontsize=14, fontweight='bold', fontstyle='italic', color=INK)
plt.savefig('figure_missing_fundamental.png', dpi=200, bbox_inches='tight')

# ── FIGURE 2 — sawtooth full | f1 removed | one octave up (each normalised) ─
plt.close('all')
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(11.2, 3.4), layout='constrained')
panels = [('sawtooth $f_1$ (C5)',         saw_full, f2,   BLUE,  'spacing 523 Hz'),
          ('sawtooth — $f_1$ removed',    saw_nof1, f2,   RED,   'spacing 523 Hz'),
          ('sawtooth — one octave up',    saw_oct,  2*f2, GREEN, 'spacing 1046 Hz')]
for j,(label,y,fp,col,sp) in enumerate(panels):
    f, P = spectrum(y); ax[j].plot(f, P/P.max(), color=col, lw=1.0)
    ax[j].axvline(fp, color=GREY, ls='--', lw=0.8)
    ax[j].set_xlim(0,5200); ax[j].set_ylim(0, 1.1)
    ax[j].set_title(label); ax[j].set_xlabel('frequency [Hz]')
    ax[j].text(0.5,0.97,sp, transform=ax[j].transAxes, ha='center', va='top', fontsize=8, color=GREY)
    ax[j].text(0.93,0.86,'♫', transform=ax[j].transAxes, ha='center', fontsize=15, color=col)
ax[0].set_ylabel('power [norm.]')
fig.suptitle('Same lowest frequency, different pitch — sawtooth without $f_1$  vs  a one-octave-up sawtooth',
             fontsize=12.5, fontweight='bold', fontstyle='italic', color=INK)
plt.savefig('figure_missing_fundamental_compare.png', dpi=200, bbox_inches='tight')

    Result

    A real middle C (C4) in three views — the waveform (period T=1/f_1 \approx 3.8 ms), the FFT magnitude (fundamental + harmonic comb), and the cepstrum (a single rahmonic peak at quefrency 1/f_1):

    Now switch to an ideal sawtooth at C5 (f_1 \approx 523 Hz), delete its fundamental, and set the result beside a sawtooth one octave up (C6). Both have their lowest energy at 2f_1 \approx 1046 Hz, but the f_1-removed sawtooth keeps a 523 Hz comb (so it is still heard as C5) while the octave-up sawtooth has a 1046 Hz comb:

    Listen for yourself — the first two are the same pitch (C5) even though the fundamental is gone from the second; the third is a genuine C6, an octave higher:

    sawtooth — C5
    f_1 \approx 523 Hz     		fundamental removed
    only \geq 2f_1 · still C5     		one octave up
    genuine C6 (2f_1 \approx 1046 Hz)

    The fundamental-removed sawtooth sounds thinner but keeps the pitch of C5 — your auditory system reconstructs the fundamental from the spacing of the surviving harmonics, which is why it is not heard as the C6 that shares its lowest frequency. The cepstrum does the same job mathematically: a single rahmonic peak at 1/f_1, robust to a missing spectral line. It is the audible counterpart of the piano-cepstrum example and the Chapter 2 treatment of the cepstrum as a “spectrum of a spectrum”.

    See Chapter 2 — Cepstrum for how the log-spectrum turns a harmonic comb into a single quefrency peak, and the Piano String Inharmonicity example for the same tone analysed for stiffness.
